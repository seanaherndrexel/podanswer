Roughly 30 downloads in the first month puts an episode near the middle of all podcasts. A couple hundred puts it in the top tenth or so, and a thousand lands somewhere near the top few percent. Those figures move around, and every source reports them a little differently, so they are bands rather than scores.

The longer answer is that the number keeping you up at night is probably not the one that decides whether your show works as a business. Most hosts arrive at this question carrying a target they absorbed from somewhere unreliable, and the target is usually wrong by an order of magnitude.

## The comparison you are making is against a ghost

The figure in your head came from a friend's show, a conference stage, or a post about a podcast with a full time producer and a network behind it. None of those describe the market you are actually competing in.

The real distribution looks nothing like that. Several million podcast feeds exist, the large majority publish rarely or stopped years ago, and among active independent shows the typical episode reaches a number of people who would fit in a school assembly. Average podcast downloads per episode, quoted as a plain average, is a misleading statistic, since a handful of enormous shows drag the mean far above anything a normal show experiences. Medians and percentiles carry much more information, which is why hosting companies report them that way.

## How many podcast downloads is good, in rough bands

Here is the shape of it, drawn from figures that podcast hosting platforms publish periodically and rounded hard on purpose. The exact numbers shift with each report, and precision here would be false comfort.

| Downloads per episode | 7 days after release | 30 days after release | Roughly where that sits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 20 | Common | Common | Bottom half of all podcasts |
| 20 to 50 | Solid start | Middle of the pack | Around the median |
| 50 to 150 | Strong | Good | Top quarter |
| 150 to 500 | Very strong | Very strong | Roughly top 10% |
| 500 to 1,500 | Unusual | Unusual | Roughly top 5% |
| 1,500 and up | Rare | Rare | Around the top 1% |

Two caveats sit under that table. An episode keeps accumulating downloads after 30 days, sometimes for years, so a back catalogue episode with 400 lifetime downloads is a different animal from a new episode with 400 in a week. Category changes the reading entirely as well. A show serving a narrow professional niche can be genuinely successful at 200 downloads an episode, because those 200 people are worth a great deal to the right sponsor, while a comedy show at 200 is struggling.

## What the counter is counting

A download is not a listener. It is an HTTP request for your audio file that ran long enough to count under the IAB measurement guidelines, which most reputable hosts follow. The practical consequences:

- One person who listens to the same episode three times on three devices can generate more than one download.
- An app that automatically downloads new episodes for a subscriber who never presses play still generates a download.
- Someone who streams 45 seconds and bails may still be counted, depending on how much of the file transferred.
- Bots and prefetching are filtered out by good hosts, imperfectly.

The number is a rough proxy for demand rather than a headcount. Two shows reporting identical downloads can have very different real audiences, which is also why comparing your host's numbers against a friend's on a different host tells you close to nothing.

## Asking how many downloads is a successful podcast is usually the wrong question

For most independent shows the download count is a lagging, low resolution summary of things you cannot act on directly. It confirms that something happened last week. It does not tell you what to change on Monday morning.

The metric also invites bad decisions. Chasing it pushes hosts toward tactics that spike the figure briefly, including paid follow campaigns and reciprocal promotion swaps, and away from the slower work that builds an audience. A goal of a bigger number this month produces a different calendar than a goal of 500 people who genuinely rely on the show in two years.

Sponsorship is the usual reason people fixate here, and the honest position is that most sponsors weigh audience quality and fit at least as heavily as raw volume. Plenty of shows under 1,000 downloads land paid deals. We walked through how that conversation actually goes in [what sponsors look at before they say yes](/blog/how-to-get-podcast-sponsors).

## The numbers that tell you something you can use

Trade the download obsession for these four, each of which points at a decision you can make this week.

**Completion rate.** How far into an episode the average listener gets. When people quit at minute four, your cold open runs long or your first segment is weak. It is the most useful number in your dashboard and most hosts now report it.

**Followers and subscribers, and the direction they are moving.** A follow is a standing instruction to deliver your next episode. Steady growth in total followers is a healthier signal than any single episode's download spike.

**Repeat listening across episodes.** Of the people who heard episode 40, how many came back for 41? That ratio tells you whether you have an audience or a run of one night stands.

**Where new listeners come from.** Without an answer to this, you cannot repeat anything that worked. Distinct traffic sources, whether search, a guest appearance or a referral from another show, are the only inputs you can deliberately increase.

## How many podcast downloads is good when your feed is three months old

Calibrate low and stay patient. Most shows in their first three months sit in the single or double digits per episode, which is a description of the starting conditions rather than a verdict on the work. A new feed has no back catalogue, no follower base, and no external pages pointing at it.

A reasonable first year looks like slow, uneven growth. You get a plateau for a few months, a bump when something outside the show sends people in, another plateau, then steadier climbing once there are enough episodes for a newcomer to binge. The pattern across the shows we work with is that hosts quit in month four, right before the catalogue gets big enough to compound. For the mechanics of that compounding, [where listeners actually come from](/blog/how-to-get-more-podcast-listeners) covers it in more detail.

## When the raw figure genuinely counts

Three situations justify caring about the download number directly. A sponsor or ad network asks for it, because that is the currency they buy in. You are comparing your own episodes against each other to see which topics land. Or you are checking for a technical fault, since a sudden drop of 40% usually means a broken feed or a redirect gone wrong rather than an audience walking out.

Outside those three cases, the figure is a scoreboard you stare at rather than a lever you pull, and the hours go further somewhere else.

## Common questions

### What is a good podcast download number for a niche show?

Lower than you think. A show serving a specific profession or hobby can be commercially viable at a couple hundred downloads an episode, because advertisers pay for relevance. The general benchmarks in the table describe all podcasts at once and do not describe your category well.

### Why do my downloads drop the week after an episode goes out?

Because most downloads for any episode arrive in the first few days, when followers' apps fetch it automatically. A steep decline after week one is the normal shape, not a problem. Look at the 30 day total instead of the daily curve.

### Should I switch hosts to get better numbers?

Numbers can shift when you move hosts because filtering methods differ slightly, but that is measurement noise rather than growth. Pick a host for reliability and analytics quality, then stop comparing across platforms.
