Most of what a podcast marketing agency sells is activity. Twelve clips posted, four audiograms rendered, a content calendar, a monthly report with impressions across the top. Some of that turns into listeners, a lot of it does not, and the invoice looks identical either way. Before you hand anyone a retainer worth $30,000 a year, it pays to know the short list of things an agency can genuinely do for a show, what each one costs, and how to tell inside a 30 minute call which kind you are talking to.

## The short menu every podcast marketing agency works from

Strip away the decks and there are four jobs on offer. Everything else is a repackaging of these.

### Getting you found by strangers

This is search: people who have never heard of you, typing a question into Google or YouTube, landing on something you made. The work covers turning episodes into text that answers real questions, building pages that can rank, treating YouTube as a search engine rather than a social feed, and cleaning up the metadata your show already has. It is the only channel where an episode you cut last spring is still bringing people in this fall.

It is also slow. Expect the first trickle in two to four months and something worth charting by six. An agency that is serious about this can tell you which questions it is targeting before it starts, not after.

### Reaching the people who already follow you

Your email list, your customers, the people who follow you somewhere and forgot you have a show. This is the cheapest listener growth available and almost nobody does it properly. An agency can put a system on it: an email per episode instead of a monthly newsletter, one consistent ask read on air, a re-engagement push to the list you have been ignoring since last year.

### Making the show worth recommending

Better cold opens, tighter edits, titles that promise something, guests who bring a reason to listen. Retention is a growth channel in its own right, because apps and YouTube both push shows that people finish. This is the least glamorous line on any proposal and frequently the most valuable thing an outside ear will ever give you.

### Making you feel promoted

Quote cards, a posting calendar, a dashboard of impressions and reach. That is a perfectly reasonable byproduct and a very expensive main product. If you cannot draw a line from a deliverable to a human being who pressed play, you are paying for the sensation of momentum.

## Deliverables are easy to promise, listeners are not

Contracts list deliverables because deliverables are the only part an agency can guarantee. Twelve clips is twelve clips whether anyone watches them or not. So the useful move is to ask which number they expect to move, and by when, before you sign anything.

Numbers that mean something:

- Downloads per episode in the first seven days, measured against your own trailing average
- New followers in Apple and Spotify month over month
- Clicks from a search result to your show page or an app
- Email signups attributable to the podcast
- Reviews left in the last 30 days

Numbers that mean very little on their own: impressions, reach, "potential audience", posts published, hours of content repurposed. If the monthly report leads with those, that is the product you bought.

Know your own baseline before the first call, because an agency will quote you against it. Our piece on [what counts as a good download number](/blog/how-many-podcast-downloads-is-good) covers where independent shows actually sit, which is lower than the pitch decks imply.

## Questions to ask before you pay a podcast marketing agency

Ask these on the first call. The answers sort the field fast.

1. Which of the four jobs above are you doing for me, and which are you not?
2. What number do you expect to move, and how long before I should see it?
3. Show me two shows in my size range, not your biggest client. What did their first six months look like?
4. Who does the work, and are they on your team or subcontracted?
5. What happens to the assets if I leave? Do the pages, the list and the accounts come with me?
6. Is there a minimum term, and what is the out?
7. How much of the fee is media spend versus labor?

The last one catches a lot of people. A $3,000 retainer that includes $2,000 of ad spend is a $1,000 service, and you should evaluate it as one.

## What podcast marketing costs when nobody is dressing it up

Prices vary by market and by how much of the work is senior time, but the bands below are close to what independent shows and small studios are quoted in the United States.

| What you are buying | Typical monthly range | Realistic time to results |
|---|---|---|
| Production help (editing, show notes, clip cutting) | $300 to $800 | Immediate, but it is labor, not marketing |
| Search and content work on the back catalog | $150 to $2,000 | 3 to 6 months, then it compounds |
| Guest booking, placements on other shows | $1,500 to $4,000 | 6 to 10 weeks to the first booking |
| Full service retainer (strategy, assets, distribution) | $2,500 to $8,000 | 3 to 6 months |
| Paid ads management | 15% to 20% of spend, plus the spend | Fast on, fast off |

One-off launch packages tend to run $3,000 to $15,000 and buy you a strong first month with nothing standing behind it. Per placement guest booking is often $300 to $1,000 a show. Advertising inside other podcasts is usually priced per thousand impressions, and rates in the $18 to $50 range are common, which makes even a modest test campaign a four figure decision rather than a two figure one.

Two things are worth holding onto. Anything under a few hundred dollars a month is buying you hours rather than outcomes, which is fine as long as you know that going in. Retainers above roughly $2,500 should come with a named strategist you can get on the phone within a day.

## How a retainer goes sideways

- **The work is front loaded.** Month one is a strategy document and an audit. Months two through twelve are the same four clips a week.
- **You are renting the audience.** Everything lives on a platform account the agency controls, or on pages that vanish when you cancel.
- **The scope drifts toward what is easy.** Search work gets replaced by social work over a couple of months, because social produces screenshots faster.
- **Nobody owns the number.** Reporting slides from downloads to impressions around month four, and that shift is the tell.
- **They will not say no.** An agency that agrees your true crime show belongs on LinkedIn is not thinking about your show.

## When keeping the money is the smarter move

Do the work yourself if your show is under about twenty episodes, if your subject is narrow enough that you personally know the twenty people who should be guests, or if you have not yet settled on a format. Agencies amplify what is already there. With nothing steady to amplify, you are paying someone to spread a thin thing thinner.

Do it yourself if your total budget sits under roughly $500 a month. At that level you can buy one specific service done well, and you should. Buying a little bit of everything gets you a little bit of nothing.

Hire when you have a back catalog worth mining, a format that works, and a bottleneck you can name out loud. "We publish weekly and nothing happens after Tuesday" is a bottleneck. "We want to grow" is a mood. ## Common questions

### How long should I give an agency before I judge it?

Six months for anything search or content based, because that is roughly when compounding work starts to show in the numbers. Eight to ten weeks for guest booking. Four weeks for paid, since paid either works quickly or does not work at all. Agree on the review date in writing at the start, so the conversation is scheduled rather than awkward.

### Is a percentage of revenue deal better than a retainer?

Sometimes, though it changes the incentive. An agency paid on ad revenue will push you toward whatever sells ads, which usually means raw download volume rather than the right listeners. If you sell a product or a service off the back of the show, a flat fee tends to keep everyone honest.

### Can an agency guarantee downloads?

Not credibly. Anyone guaranteeing a specific download number is either buying the traffic, in which case it stops when the spend stops, or counting something loosely. A good agency will commit to a volume of work and to a review of the numbers, never to the numbers themselves.
